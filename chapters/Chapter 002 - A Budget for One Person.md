# Chapter 2: A Budget for One Person

Deciding what they meant by connected took longer than any measurement.

"There are three different jobs here," Zorian said. "Mara wants the works office to stop treating her readings as the mistake. I want to know whether those two stones are joined to something. Miss Zileti wants a report she can sign without lying. Those are not the same task, and if we pretend otherwise we will produce a document that satisfies nobody."

Mara put her pencil down. "That is the most useful thing anyone has said to me since yesterday."

"Then let us do it badly and quickly."

Ilsa had not moved from the doorway while he talked. She had arrived ten minutes earlier, taken the drawing from the table, and stood with it for a long time. Imaya had offered her tea in the hallway and Ilsa had accepted, which meant she intended to stay for a while. She had not once asked whether it was all right for a student to be present.

"It is a matched pair," she said now. "That is the first thing. The two curves belong to each other. Whoever cut them was making one object out of two halves."

"You are certain?" Mara asked.

"I am certain that they face each other, that they have the same depth of cut, and that the second one was interrupted where your new collar begins. Beyond that I am guessing." Ilsa turned the drawing so Mara could see the gaps Zorian had left. "He has done this properly. He has drawn what was visible and refused to invent the rest."

"My name is on this?"

"Your name is not on it. It is a drawing made at my request by a student assisting with a diagnostic." Ilsa set it down. "Which is a thing I am willing to say out loud, and the only qualification I am willing to sign."

Kael looked up from his fittings list. "So nobody is certifying him."

"Nobody is certifying anybody," Ilsa said. "The works office has a diagnostic allowance and a rule that the allowance may only be spent on an authorized party. I am an authorized party for reconstruction wardwork. I have agreed to accept a commission covering six cellars, one of which is currently locked by the watch."

Mara said, "The allowance is capped. Fifty gold for the whole survey."

"I know. I read the schedule." Ilsa's expression suggested that the schedule had not been a pleasant read. "The commission covers measurement, documentation and recommendations. It does not cover repairs, it does not cover any authority to open a site, and it does not cover anyone's opinion about what the stones are for."

"That is a short list of what you are allowed to do," Mara said.

"It is a long list of what I refuse to be responsible for. There is a difference, and it is why crews get to go home at night." Ilsa took the tea from Imaya's tray without looking. "The works office will send its own reader to repeat anything we intend to act on. That is not an insult to the method. It is the method. If your reading survives being taken by a man who dislikes you, then it is a reading."

"My reading will survive it," Mara said, "because I wrote down the settings while I was taking it."

Zorian turned her sheet around and read the column again. She had recorded which probe face had touched the copper, the distance of the instrument from the stone, the state of the supply key, and the time. She had also written a line saying that she did not know what the older channels were.

"You wrote that last part before you found out anyone cared," he said.

"I write that part whenever it is true."

* * *

The packet stayed where it was on the table. Kael looked at it the way he looked at things he wanted and had decided not to touch.

"I can tell you what the crust is made of," he said. "Not how old it is. The basement has a burner, some glass, and whatever survived being blown up. The new building has a furnace, and the furnace has a permit problem."

"Explain the permit problem," Zorian said.

"Miss Kuroshka will not have alchemy under her floor. I am not doing alchemy under her floor, I am doing an assay of a mineral crust, which is a different activity in every way except the important one." Kael pushed his list away. "Give me two days in the new place with a hot burner and I will tell you whether that crust grew onto the copper or the copper was laid over the crust. That much does not need a permit, because it needs a furnace, and we do not have one yet."

"Then the crust waits," Mara said.

"The crust will wait. The crust has been waiting for years. It is extremely good at waiting."

Zorian wrote that question down in the place where he kept the questions that could not be answered yet. There were several of them now, arranged in a rough column, and the column had begun to look like a ledger of his own ignorance.

Ilsa finished her tea. "Where are we going first?"

"The locked one," Mara said.

"The locked one it is."

* * *

Before that he had to do the unglamorous half of the morning, which was the part that had been waiting for him since he woke up.

The study group had moved to the front room, where the table was too small and the children were under it. Ilsa was demonstrating something with a candle and a clay dish, and Edwin was writing it down badly, and Naim was telling him that his handwriting had improved greatly over the past year, which Edwin believed.

Akoja was at the window with a board on her knee. She had always kept the group's records, and since the invasion she had been keeping them with an extra page for attendance, as though she suspected that the number of people in a room was a form of safety equipment.

"Imaya wants a count for meals," Zorian said.

"I know. I gave her one yesterday and she said it was wrong." Akoja turned the board around. "Eleven of us come most mornings. Twelve when Kael eats with us instead of in the basement, which is often, because he forgets. Ilsa comes four days and sometimes brings a man from the wardworks who eats nothing and talks a great deal. Kirielle, Nochka and Kana eat whenever they are here, which is not a fixed number. Imaya wants it by noon so that she can buy."

"Then give her eleven plus Kael plus Ilsa and tell her that the children are hers, because she will know what she is doing."

"She will know what she is doing," Akoja agreed. "She also wants to know whether you are eating at home in the evenings, because there is a butcher's woman who will sell a shoulder cheap on Thursday if someone commits to it on Wednesday."

"I will be here on Thursday."

"Then I will tell her you are here on Thursday," Akoja said, and wrote it in, which was how the household acquired things.

Imaya came in with a pot and stopped the conversation the way she stopped conversations, by holding out a spoon and asking someone to taste it. Zorian tasted it. It was too salty and he said so, and she said that she had been waiting for him to say so. Then she took the board off Akoja's knee, read the number on it out loud, eleven with Kael and Ilsa written beside it in two different hands, and copied it into the small book she kept in her apron without breaking the thread of what she was saying about the butcher's woman. When she had finished she asked him about his evening.

"I need the next two evenings," he said. "Possibly three. I am working for Miss Zileti on a diagnostic near the tanners' line."

"Which is a thing I am glad to hear," Imaya said, "if I hear it before I put the pot on, and not after."

"That is why I am telling you now."

"You are telling me now, and I am writing it on the board by the door, and if something changes, you send a boy rather than relying on me to guess. I have eleven mouths in this house and one of them is a child who will eat nothing but bread if I let her." She looked at Kirielle, who was under the table and stated that this was not true. "You are going to be paid?"

"Yes."

"Then write down what you are paid. Not for me. For whoever comes after you wanting to know." She turned back to her pot. "I have been keeping the accounts of this house for nineteen years and the useful thing about a written number is that it remembers even when nobody else does."

That, at least, he could do.

* * *

He did it at the kitchen table after the front room emptied out, with a clean sheet and the back of his anchor drawing pushed aside.

The room and board he paid Imaya was a straightforward line: eleven silver a month, plus whatever the household put in for coal, plus the extra he owed since two more students had arrived in the spare room and the washwoman's rate had gone up. The study group's coffee and bread were another small sum that he had never written down, because it was small, which was the usual way that small sums became a habit.

Then came the harder column.

His savings were large. That had been true for a year, and it had been true in a stranger way during the invasion, when every month had ended with the same number because it had been the same month, and when he had never once had to decide whether he could afford a thing twice.

The amount he had with him was still enough to make him feel calm for about four seconds at a time. Then he did the arithmetic of a month and the calm moved on to someone else.

He wrote the monthly rate he was paying for the yard where Mrva lay in pieces. Nine gold, for a walled plot at the edge of the outer quarter with a roofed cart shed over the worst of it and a caretaker named Boleslavin who was honest about two things and asleep about the rest. Boleslavin had sent a note that week, in the shape of a question he would have preferred not to ask: whether the big stones on the ground were going to be moved before spring, because his boy had hurt his hand on one, and whether the fence wards needed to be renewed, because nobody had renewed them.

The renewal was quoted at twenty-six gold. A properly warded store, with an alarm he would be able to hear from inside the city and a wall a thief had to climb twice, was quoted at something over three hundred, and then a warden's wage on top of it. He had the numbers written on a card in his coat from a conversation two months ago in which a ward engineer had laughed at him for asking.

Mrva himself was another sum. The cheapest honest estimate for a restoration of the golem's locomotion and frame had been given to him by Edwin, who had done it carefully, and the figure had been roughly four times the cost of the wall he could not afford.

Zorian put the card away and did not add that line to the sheet. He would add it when he had something to put next to it.

The herders' claim arrived with the morning post, carried by a boy who wanted two coppers for the trouble. It was a formal paper, in a careful hand, from three men living at the northern edge of the Great Forest. A cow and two goats were missing, and horses had been injured when a flock of large birds had come over their pasture, and all of it was said in the flat manner of people who had already decided that the rich were going to cheat them and were trying to get ahead of it. They asked for forty-five silver. They also asked, in a second paragraph, whether the beasts belonged to a mage of the Kazinski name, because if they did not, they were going to speak to the border guard instead.

He read the second paragraph twice.

Princess was in the forest. The iron beaks were in the forest, following her, making her into something that could be seen from a pasture and noticed by a guard. It was not an emergency. It was not going to kill anyone this week. It was exactly the kind of problem he could put in a drawer, and the drawer in question had a name and the name was next month.

He wrote the claim on the sheet, and under it he wrote: the forest, and then a date, and then stopped, because a date without a plan was a wish.

Then the income column, which he had not written in before.

He tried it first with what he had actually been doing: making things and selling them through people who asked few questions. He had done that before, and it had produced two results. The first was money, which was good. The second was that a merchant in the silk quarter had begun asking who the maker was, and Kopriva had told him at dinner that a man had asked her, in the street, in a friendly way, where she had learned his name. That had been several weeks ago and nothing had come of it, which was not the same thing as nothing happening.

He wrote the rule he wanted, and the rule was small enough to be embarrassing: one buyer, not the same item twice, no commissions, and nothing whose design could not be explained by a competent third-year student with good tools. It cost him something to write it down, because it meant the money he could make on purpose was smaller than the money he could make if he stopped caring.

Then he wrote the allowance. Fifty gold for the whole survey, of which he would see a fraction as a day rate, for two days of kneeling in cellars. He did the sum and put the number in his income column, where it looked like what it was: not a solution, but the first line in a column that had to start somewhere.

At the end he wrote the thing he had been avoiding, in the middle of the page, where he would have to look at it.

Forty gold a month. That was the cap he was setting on himself: food, household, reagents, paper, his own training, the small things, everything he wanted. Anything above it had to come from money he could name out loud in front of somebody, and if he could not name it, he did not get to spend it.

He looked at the line for a long time.

For a year he had planned in months he could get back. Now he was planning a month he could not, and it felt like writing on paper that would burn. The habit of a hundred restarts did not give way to a number on a sheet just because the number was honest.

He folded the sheet and put it in the front pocket of his coat, where it would stay, so that he would have to carry the decision instead of leaving it on the table.

* * *

Raynie was in the hallway with her bag by the door and Kiana sitting on the bottom step, doing nothing in an aggressive manner.

He had not heard her go downstairs. That was a small piece of evidence, and he made himself look at it, because the household was crowded and full of people who had learned to move around each other at speed, and it was not the sort of thing that should have made his chest go tight.

"You are going now," he said.

"The cart leaves at the second bell." She had packed neatly, which was unlike her, and she had packed twice, because she had taken things out and put them back. "I have been standing here deciding whether to take the good ink, and I have decided to take the good ink, and now I have two bottles."

"Take both."

"I cannot carry both and a bag."

"Kiana can carry one."

"I can not," Kiana said from the step, with great feeling, and then, after a moment, "give it here."

Raynie handed her the ink.

"There are two letters," she said. "I have them and I would like you to know about them, because you are the one person in this house who will tell me if I am being stupid."

"Then I will tell you if you are being stupid. That is my stated function."

"The cat shifters sent an account." She had the letters in the pocket of her coat, and she did not take them out. "Itemized. Rope, four horses, two days of runners, the use of the south yard, and a man's wages for the day my brother was found. They priced the horses at what a horse costs now, which is a great deal, and they priced the runners by the day, which is fair, and they expect it paid by me, because I asked them, and they did not ask anyone at home first."

"That is a real debt," Zorian said carefully. "It is not your debt alone."

"I know that. That is the problem. If I pay it, then I am the kind of person who promises things on behalf of our family, and if I do not pay it, then I am the kind of person who does not pay." She turned the bag strap over her fingers. "My uncle wrote to say that he hopes I understand what I have done. He did not say what he thought I had done, so I have to guess, and I have had two weeks to guess."

"And the second letter?"

"The pigeon shifters want me to speak at their gathering in the autumn, in my family's name, about the rescue." Raynie's mouth did something that was not a smile. "They have already told people I would. That is not a debt of money. It is worse, because it is a favor, and a favor has no amount on it."

Zorian thought about the numbers on the sheet in his pocket, and about forty-five silver from three herders, and about how a debt could be smaller than a favor and still be easier to settle. He did not say any of it.

"There is a way to answer the first letter," he said. "Not by paying it. By writing what was actually owed to whom, and sending a copy to both your uncle and the runner's family, so that the amount is in the open and belongs to more than one person. It is slower than paying, and less satisfying."

"That sounds like something you would say."

"I have been settling accounts with people who do not like each other for a while now."

"It sounds like something you would say," Raynie repeated, in a much softer voice, and then she laughed once, which helped. "All right. I will write it before I get there, and I will read it badly and badly at my aunt afterwards. And if the police ask you anything about the rescue, you tell me before you tell them, because I would like to hear it from you first and not from a newspaper."

"Yes."

"And if you write to me, do not write about spiders and negotiations as though I have been keeping up. I have not been keeping up. I have been in a tent."

"I will write about the household."

"That would be nice," she said.

The second bell rang somewhere off in the direction of the north gate, and Raynie's shoulders lifted at it in a small, involuntary way.

"You have time for the second cart," Zorian said. "There is one an hour later."

"There is." She looked at the door, and then at him, and then she put her hand briefly on his sleeve, which was not the sort of gesture that meant anything in a hallway and also was. "I will be back when the Academy opens. Unless my family decides that the Academy is a thing I used to do."

"Then I will write to the tent," he said, "and ask."

She and Kiana went out with the bag and the good ink, Kiana as far as the corner and no further, because she had errands and said so loudly at the corner. The hallway was cold for a few seconds, and Imaya shouted from the kitchen that the door, and Zorian closed it.

* * *

The locked cellar was doing nothing at all, according to the watch.

They had to wait at the rope for a watch mage with a clipboard, who arrived with an expression that had been prepared for a difficult morning and found a harder one. Ilsa showed him a paper with the Academy's seal on it and the works office's reference number under it. He read it twice.

"You are aware," he said, "that this site is closed by order of the works office, and I am supposed to keep everyone away from it, including mages."

"You are supposed to keep unqualified people away from it," Ilsa said. "Write my name on your sheet and note that the commission is on site, then stand where I can see you and do not be unpleasant to the surveyor. She is the person whose job you have made worse."

"I have not made anyone's job worse," the watch mage said, but he wrote the names.

Dovin was sitting on an upturned crate by the rope with his left arm in a sling and his right hand on a mug. He had the look of a man who had been at home for four hours and decided that home was a place where nothing was going to happen to him.

"You asked for the morning off," Mara said.

"I asked for the morning off and I got it. Nobody said anything about the afternoon." He stood up with the care of somebody testing his own ankle, and offered his free hand to Zorian. "You are the one who held it while she lifted. I was going to thank you properly and then find out who turned that wheel, and in that order."

"You did not touch it," Zorian said.

"I did not touch it, and I would like to be the one standing next to it when the office's man asks me again." He looked at Mara. "There's a thing people say, that a man who is pinned will say anything to get out from under. I want to be here when she asks."

"Then stay by the rope and hold the mug," Mara said, and her voice was much less dry than it had been all morning. "And if you are going to be difficult, be difficult slowly."

They went down in the order that the space allowed: Mara with her instrument, Ilsa with a brass box the size of a hat, and Zorian last with a lamp and a folded sheet of paper. Kael arrived separately, out of breath, carrying a satchel that he had refused to leave at the house, and a stopwatch that he said was accurate to within a minute, which he said in the tone of a man hoping nobody would ask him to prove it.

The plan was Ilsa's, and it was good in the plain way that experienced people plan things.

They would take the record at the first site without anyone standing in it. Ilsa's brass box was a recorder, with a stylus and a plate driven by clockwork, and it had no will and no opinion and had not been within thirty feet of any of them while it worked. They wound it, set it on the floor three paces from the north anchor, and everybody went up to the street.

The watch mage was told to watch the recorder and touch nothing. He was asked to do this in a voice that made it clear she expected him to say no and was glad when he did not.

For six minutes they stood in the street and talked about nothing. Zorian stood with his back against a wall, at the mouth of the stairs, and did not cast, and did not reach for anything. The street's mana moved as it always did: the mains, the shop wards, the frame's bleeding reserve. Beneath that, faint, patient, the old line rose.

He wrote nothing down. That was the discipline. His senses were not evidence, and the reason they were not evidence was not that other people were stupid. It was that a thing he felt and could not show was a thing that would be argued about in a room full of people with jobs, and the arguing would eat the week, and meanwhile the crews waiting at the other four cellars would still be waiting.

They went down. The plate had a trace on it: a flat line, then a rise, then a smaller rise, then the flat line again, at the same place on the plate every time.

Mara read it out loud, and Ilsa wrote what she said, and nobody said that it matched anything, because there was nothing yet to match it to.

Then Kael went to the second site with a second recorder, and Zorian and Mara took the readings by hand with Mara's copper instrument, and Ilsa stood at the top of the stairs and asked them each question twice, once in the order she expected and once in the order she did not.

At the second site the supply had never been connected. The new copper collar was in place, because the collar came from a crate and a collar does not carry a current by itself. Nothing was below it but the stone, which had been down there longer than the street.

Zorian held the probe and read the needles, and Mara wrote, and then she held the probe and read her own instrument and wrote, and the numbers were close enough to be the same numbers, which was worth more than either of them wanted to admit.

And the order was still reversed.

The recorder at the second site agreed. So did the hand readings, taken by two people who had never met each other a day ago and who disagreed about whether the office's reader was an honest man.

"Could it be your collar?" Ilsa asked. "The copper, sitting there, ringing like a bell."

"The copper does not ring," Mara said. "And the older inscription does not run under the collar at this site. Look." She had the drawing board out, and she laid her finger on the stone's flank. "At the first cellar they cut the channels straight through the old curves to fix the collar. Here the collar stops two inches short, because the crew hit something hard and argued about the cost. That is in my notes, and it is in the crew's notes, because they complained to me about it for a week."

Ilsa looked at the stone, and then at the drawing, and then she sat down on the cellar stair with the board across her knees, which was the first time all morning that she had looked pleased rather than careful.

"There is something I have not told anybody," she said. "I have seen this shape twice, in the past three weeks, and I assumed I was looking at badly copied load work. Once in a culvert under the tanners' gate, where the old cutting is in the brick and the new culvert is inside it. Once in a granary cellar near the old market, where the same two curves, in the same relation to each other, are cut into a wall that is not carrying anything at all."

Mara turned her map around.

Six cellars, in a line, and the accident marked with a cross.

"Say it," Mara said.

"I am not going to say it until I have a third and a fourth," Ilsa said. "I will say this: your map is a line of streets, and one of the places I have seen is not on it. If the stones in your six cellars and the wall in that granary are the same work, then somebody laid a pattern across this district, and it is old, and it is bigger than any one of these houses, and every crew in Cyoria is currently drilling into it and calling it a footing."

* * *

The works office sent a reader at the end of the afternoon, because Ilsa had said Friday and the office had said she could have Wednesday or nothing.

He was a thin man with a good instrument and a bad morning. He took the readings at the first site himself, in the order he preferred, with the watch mage holding the supply key and Dovin sitting on his crate with his mug, and when the trace came up he asked for it again and got it again, and then he sat on the crate next to Dovin and said, in a very quiet voice, "What is under my street?"

"That," said Mara, "is what the survey is for."

He wrote in his book for a while with his jaw set, and then he did the thing that Zorian had learned to expect from officials when they were frightened rather than angry: he made a rule.

Unloading at the four sound sites could resume, because four crews had families and a schedule, and stopping them would create a different danger. But no site was to have its supply reconnected, no anchor was to be moved, and no work was to touch anything older than the new fitting. If a stone answered twice, the crew was to stop, mark it, and send a runner to the office before anyone's boot went down a stair.

"That is my authority," he said to Ilsa, "and it is the office's, and neither of us likes it. I am writing that the anomaly is unexplained and that the diagnostic is ongoing, and if anyone higher up asks me to make that phrase shorter, I will ask them to come and hold the plate."

He gave Mara a paper that restored her authority at the four sites, conditional on a witness being present at every unloading, and he gave her a second paper that was a receipt. Before that he agreed, in writing and with visible reluctance, to go back on Friday and repeat the second site with Mara holding the probe, because he did not trust a reading he had not taken himself and was honest enough not to pretend that he did.

"Fifty gold against the commission," he said, "which is a survey, and the survey is not finished, and if the survey turns out to be a diagnosis I will have to argue with a different office about a different budget. The day rate goes to whoever was in the cellars, and I have the Academy's paper for that, which is where your money comes from, miss."

Ilsa paid them at the top of the stairs, out of her own purse, with a recklessness that suggested she intended to win that argument later. Three gold a day for Zorian, and Kael, who had held the recorder at the second site and written his own times, asked for four and got it without a fight, which he mentioned twice.

Zorian's receipt was a small square of paper with his name on it and a number, and he stood in the street holding it longer than he meant to.

It was not a great deal of money. It was money that had been agreed in front of a witness, at a rate someone had written down, for work that another man had checked, and it was the first such sum he had earned in a very long time. He put it in the front pocket with the folded sheet, where the two pieces of paper pressed together, and he went home in the early dark with the taste of cellar in his teeth and the feeling of a man who has been given a very small permission.

He sent a message with a neighbor's boy before the street lamps were lit, because that was what Imaya had asked for, and she had written the evenings on the board by the door and left a space for the word late, which the boy filled in with a pencil he was not supposed to have.

* * *

Kirielle was waiting in the yard with the thread wound around her finger and a chair she had dragged outside, which was not the household's best decision and was also the sort of thing he would have done at her age.

"You said today," she said. "You said it this morning, so it is still today."

"I said today."

"You are late."

"I am late," he agreed. "We have ten minutes, and then we eat, and if you are good about the ten minutes I will show you why the lamp matters."

That bought him the yard.

The exercise was the same one from the morning, but smaller: a thread of guided mana, drawn out to the length of her forearm, with one end held and the other allowed to follow. Her failure that morning had come from trying to hold the entire thing at once, like somebody gripping a rope with both hands and then complaining that none of it was moving. Now he asked her to hold only near her hand and let the far end go slack, and the far end went slack, and then she got excited about it and pulled, and the thread snapped.

"Again," she said immediately, and then, when it snapped again, "that is a stupid exercise."

"It is a rope," he said. "You are learning which end you own."

"I own both ends. It is my mana."

"You own the end you are touching. That is the whole spell, and the whole problem, and it is also why nobody can teach you to be gentle by describing gentleness." He shifted the chair and put a lamp on it, low, so that the yard's dim light had something to compare against. "Try it toward the light. The thread is thin enough that you will see the shadow it throws when it is steady, and you will see nothing at all when you jerk it."

She tried it toward the light, and stopped, and stared, and then held perfectly still, and the shadow appeared: a hairline across the stones, moving slightly, the way a blade of grass moves.

"There," she said, in a voice with no room in it for any comment whatsoever.

"There," Zorian said.

The lamp went out because Nochka, who had been sitting behind it, sat down on it.

Kirielle shouted at her, Nochka shouted back, and the shadow was gone, and the yard was only a yard again. Then Kana arrived from the washyard with water in her sandals and told them that the food was being called in, and Kirielle went in first, and stopped, and turned around in the doorway.

"I held it," she said.

"You did."

"For a whole long time."

"For about twenty counts, which is four times this morning." She had her chin up, daring him to make it smaller.

He did not make it smaller. "Next is twenty counts while somebody talks to you, which is harder, and you will hate it."

"I do not hate it," she said, and went inside to wash her hands, and did not slam the door, which was almost the same thing.

* * *

Later, in the room he shared with a sleeping child and a snow globe, Zorian put the sheet from that morning flat on the table and set his receipt next to it.

Six cellars in a line. One trace with the strong peak first and one with the strong peak last, taken by two instruments and two people, on a stone that had never been connected to anything. A wall at a granary that was carrying nothing. A culvert under a gate, where the old cutting was inside the new one, meaning that whatever was down there had been there before anybody thought of putting a street on top of it.

He wrote the Friday repeat at the second site into his list, and the office's two rules, because Imaya was right that a written number remembers things. Then, underneath the money column, in the place where a plan is either a plan or a wish, he wrote the words he had refused to write at noon.

The forest. Before the grass comes up.

The last line he added in smaller letters, at the bottom of the page, where nobody would see it and ask what he meant:

Something old under six cellars, and it is still working.
