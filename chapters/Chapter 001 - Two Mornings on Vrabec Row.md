# Chapter 1: Two Mornings on Vrabec Row

Zorian was awake before the household was, which happened so rarely that he had begun to treat it as weather. Something had gone wrong with the dawn. He lay still in his own bed with his own ceiling above him and listened to Cyoria through the window: a cart with a cracked wheel, gulls arguing over the canal, a boy shouting a bread price three streets away. Under all of it, in the back of his skull, the clock he had built out of nothing but practice was telling him that it was twenty past five, and that it had been telling him twenty past five for considerably longer than it should have.

He had stopped trusting that clock the way he had stopped trusting his own reflection. Both had been useful during the month, and both had come back from it wrong. He rolled over, did the arithmetic of how many hours he had left before the day began eating him alive, and decided that he had time for an hour of work in the yard if he did not waste twenty minutes on the stairs.

Kirielle was asleep on top of his stomach, which was a problem she had solved without consulting him. She had grown again over the summer and her elbows had found new and creative places to put themselves. She had also taken to moving things onto his chest while he slept, so that now his shirt held a stolen spoon, a ribbon, and a small brass weight he recognized as one of his paperweights.

He considered waking her. He considered the alternative. There was, technically, an argument for the alternative: he could leave quietly, take his work down to the yard, and be back before anyone noticed that his sister had made a nest of him. He had made that calculation and had begun, very carefully, to lift the spoon, when the spoon turned out to be a trap.

"You're awake," Kirielle said into his shirt.

"I was awake a while ago," Zorian said. "You are on my lungs."

"You snore like a dog that's angry at a second dog." She rolled off him with the full-body commitment of a person who had slept extremely well, sat up, and rearranged her hair into something that was not a solution but was at least an attempt. "There's a man at the shop and he wants to sell the big thing."

The big thing was Mrva, or rather what was left of him: the torso and arm-casing of a colossus that had once been the most reliable thing standing between Cyoria and its enemies, taken apart for storage because no shed in this city was big enough to hold it whole, and laid out in the yard behind Imaya's house under tarps that were three winters old. The heap had not been for sale in any sense of the phrase since the day it had stopped working, and Zorian had said so, clearly and at length, to the man who had come to look at it.

"What kind of man."

"A soft one," Kirielle said, with the contempt children reserve for adults who are soft about the wrong things. "He said he could do you a kindness. Imaya is trying to make him leave without making him leave."

Zorian sat up, and the small brass weight fell into his lap. On the wall by the window, on a nail he had put in himself during the loop out of a sentimentality he would not have admitted to at the time, hung a scrap of paper with a list he had written for himself and never shown anybody. He did not read lists like that in the morning. He put on his trousers.

* * *

Imaya's house had been a six-person household with a quiet kitchen and one long table, and the war had made it into something else. The Academy dormitories had taken artillery in the first hour and a fistfight in the second, and by the time the city had stopped burning, the tenants of Imaya's house had multiplied in the way that people multiply when there is an empty room and no one to ask about it. There were two third-years in the front room, a girl from the eastern provinces who slept with her wand under her pillow, a Koth-trained artificer's apprentice in the attic who paid for his room by mending the plumbing, and Kopriva, who had no need of a room at all and had taken the second-best one anyway, on the grounds that the best one was drafty and the third-best had a view of a wall.

The Academy had warded the house against intrusion, which was a courtesy extended to a building it was using as an improvised classroom. It had also, in the process of doing so, installed a bell by the side door that announced everyone who came and went, and it had installed that bell badly, so that it rang when someone opened the door and, on wet mornings, when nobody did.

That morning the bell rang four times before Zorian was halfway down the stairs, and each ring was followed by the specific brand of silence that means a conversation has been interrupted by a man in his shirtsleeves.

He passed the board by the door. Imaya kept it there, a plank of pine on two nails, and on it she had written the week with chalk in a hand that improved as it went downhill. Evening meals were promised in the left column. The names of the people who owed them were in the right column. There was a third column that said "and don't," which had two names in it, one of which was his, under the heading of "the thing with the lamps."

It was an excellent system, and he had broken it twice in a single week, and the household had not yet decided whether that made him a bad influence or merely a dangerous one.

Downstairs, the kitchen was doing what it always did, which was feeding more people than it had been built for out of a single pot. Imaya stood at the stove with her back to the room and a wooden spoon in her hand, and the soft man from the shop was standing by the table with his hat in his hands and the particular posture of a person who has decided that kindness is a form of argument.

"You're up," Imaya said, without turning around. "Sit down. Eat. And then you're going to tell me what you told that man, because he looked at me like I was the one lying."

"I told him the golem is not for sale," Zorian said. "I told him the golem is also not fixable by anybody who owns a cart, and I told him the price of the parts would feed this house for a year, which I said to discourage him."

"And he wasn't discouraged."

"He asked if I'd consider a trade."

Imaya turned around with the spoon still in her hand and gave the soft man the look that had, at one point in her career, made hardened mercenaries reconsider their plans at a distance. The soft man did not notice, because he was a man who had built a whole career on not noticing, and so Imaya did the only thing available to her. She gave him a bowl.

"Sit," she said. "You're here now."

He sat. He ate. He was still eating twenty minutes later, slowly, with the air of a man who understood that he was being beaten and did not know by what.

Akoja was at the long table with her elbows in somebody's porridge, going through a stack of Academy notices with a pencil, and she did not look up as Zorian came in.

"The dormitories are not going to be finished before term," she said. "Ilsa's been told and Ilsa has not told anybody, which is why Ilsa is going to be furious later instead of now. Also there's a fee increase for non-resident students, which is why Kopriva is going to be furious instead. Also the third-year placement review is back, and this time it's run by a board instead of by whichever instructor is least able to avoid it."

"That's useful," Zorian said.

"It's four pages of useful," Akoja said, and turned the stack around so that he could read the heading of the top one. "I copied the important bits. Three lines. You can manage three lines."

He could, in fact, manage three lines, and the three lines said that the Academy was reopening on schedule, that the third-year cohort would be reviewed for advancement at the end of the first month, and that a student who could demonstrate the required competence in more than one discipline could be advanced into the fourth-year courses of that discipline without sitting the third year again.

He read the third line twice, and the soft man with the bowl looked at him hopefully, and Kopriva, coming down the stairs in his stocking feet, said, "Oh no. He's found a loophole."

"There isn't a loophole," Zorian said. "There's a rule with a condition in it."

"That's what a loophole looks like from the inside," Kopriva said. "I've looked at a lot of them."

* * *

The lesson was in the yard because Imaya did not want any more spells indoors, and because the yard was the only part of the property where the sound of failure was not going to wake anybody. The ground was wet, and the little square of chalk that Kirielle had drawn for herself was going grey, and she was standing in the middle of it with her shoulders up around her ears in the way that meant she was about to be bad at something and was annoyed at being expected to be seen doing it.

"It's a light," she said. "It's the light cantrip. Everybody's five-year-old does the light cantrip."

"Five-year-olds have had six years of practice at being bored and it suits them," Zorian said. "Show me yours."

Hers was a pale smear of brightness around her right fist that gave off no heat, cast no useful shadow, and made her knuckles look mildly swollen. It also wobbled, and it also went out when she swore at it, and when it went out she stood there with her fist still raised in the air, as if she could intimidate her own mana into returning.

"That's not a light," Zorian said. "That's the shadow of one, badly drawn. What are you holding?"

"A line," Kirielle said, in the voice of a person reciting something that had been shouted at her. "You said to hold the shape of a line with a bend at the end and then let the light come from the bend instead of from me, and I did, and it doesn't work, and Raynie says the Academy teaches it as a sphere because spheres are easier and the Academy teaches everything as a sphere because the Academy is run by old men who like spheres."

Raynie, who was leaning against the shed in her traveling cloak with her arms folded and had been there for some time, said, "I said nothing about old men."

"You said spheres are easier and the Academy is run by men who like spheres," Zorian said. "I have been translating you."

"It's true," Raynie said, "and also she's wrong about the shape."

"She's wrong about the shape because I taught her the wrong shape on purpose," Zorian said.

Kirielle's fist had gone down to her side. She looked at him with the specific fury of a child who has just discovered that an adult has been lying to her for her own benefit, which is the first truly useful thing anybody learns about adults.

"A line with a bend is how a real light is shaped," Zorian said, "and it's difficult, and you've been holding a difficult shape badly for a week, which is why your hand aches and your light is a smear. A sphere is easy, and I'm not going to teach you the easy one first, because the easy one teaches you to hold a shape with your fists instead of with your wrist, and I'm not going to spend the next two years un-teaching you."

"Then why didn't you say so."

"Because you'd have believed me instead of finding it out, and finding it out is the lesson."

"That's a stupid lesson."

"It's the only one I have," Zorian said. "Again. Wrist. Bend. And don't try to make the light. Make the bend, and then tell me what the bend wants."

She did it again, and this time she did it correctly, and the light that came out of the bend was small, and steady, and white, and it held itself in the air an arm's length away from her with a faintly blue edge, and it lit up the wet yard well enough that she could see the chalk line under her feet, and she stared at it so hard that she forgot, temporarily, to be pleased with herself.

"There," Zorian said.

"That took a week."

"That took a week and it will stay taken," he said. "Now do it with your other hand and do not talk while you do it, because when you talk you spend the mana you were going to spend on the shape and I want you to notice that you're doing it."

She did it with her other hand. It was worse, and then it was better, and then she was grinning at the yard, and the grinning was the part that cost her the shape, and the light went out, and this time she laughed instead of swearing, which was progress of a different and rarer kind.

"Zorian," Kirielle said. "Can I do the one that makes the water jump?"

"No."

"You don't even know what I meant."

"I know exactly what you meant and the answer is no until your wrist stops shaking, because a jumping stream of water is a knife that hasn't been aimed," he said. "Next week, maybe. If you can hold a bend in both hands for a count of twenty without losing it, we'll look at moving something small and dry."

"That's not what I asked for."

"It's what you need," Zorian said. "Go help Imaya with the pots, you've earned a place at the table."

Kirielle went. Raynie did not, which meant that she had not come to deliver a message and had come instead to watch, which was the sort of thing Raynie did and did not explain.

"You're teaching her the hard order," Raynie said.

"I'm teaching her the order I learned in, which happens to be the hard one, because the man who taught me was a monster with excellent technique."

"And if she cries."

"Then she cries, and I sit with her while she does, and we start again tomorrow," Zorian said. "That's the whole method. It's not a secret and it isn't complicated, it's just expensive in time, and time is the one thing I've never had enough of."

Raynie looked across the yard, where the tarp had lifted at the corner and was flapping against a curved plate of metal as wide as a door, and where one detached hand sat upright against the wall like something waiting to be called, and then she looked at him, and her face did something quick and unreadable, and then she said, "You need money."

"I need a very specific and enormous amount of money, which is worse."

"Come to the auction," Raynie said. "Bring the parts you don't need. Bring me, I'll shout at people for you. There's a salvage sale on Friday and the houses that lost their cellars are selling everything they can't put back, which means there's going to be a lot of good metal going cheap to men who don't know what they're holding."

"I don't want the metal," Zorian said. "I want the enchantments to still be on it, which is exactly what they won't be."

"Then come and look before they're stripped," Raynie said, and pushed off the shed, and added over her shoulder, "And bring your sister. She's the only person in this house who can haggle by being disappointing, and that's a skill."

* * *

The letter arrived with the post at half past nine, addressed to him, sealed with the Koth end's office mark, and it was not written by him, which is to say that it was written by him, and not by the one of him who had opened it.

He read it standing up by the kitchen table, while the household went around him.

The copy in Koth had the fourth number, which was a number that had once meant something and now meant only that he had started counting and had not been able to bring himself to stop. The handwriting was his own, and it had drifted, and this version of his handwriting leaned further left than the original ever had, so that the letter looked like his own notes being slowly pulled sideways by the wind.

Four wrote that the gate inspection schedule had changed twice in a month and the adepts had begun charging for the transport of personal correspondence, which was a small insult but an instructive one. Four wrote that he had spent eleven days in the Koth end's salvage archive doing the inventory job Zorian had set him, and that he had finished it, and that he would like to be told what he was supposed to be doing now, because he had begun making up work and had noticed himself doing it and did not like the way it felt. Four wrote that he had taken a room near the Silent Doorway's house rather than sleeping in the archive, because the archive was cold and because he had been offered the room by a family who did not know what he was and who would want to know, and that this was the sort of thing that ought to be decided jointly rather than by him alone, which is why he was writing instead of asking.

And at the bottom, in a hand that had begun to lean, Four had written one line that was not a report: he wanted to be allowed to take the wedding preparations seriously, and to be told whether anyone intended to ask him to the ceremony, or whether the fact that he was a part of somebody else's family was going to be treated as a detail.

Zorian read that line three times, standing up, while Imaya stacked bowls and put a hand on the back of his neck as she passed, which was her way of asking what was wrong without asking it in front of witnesses.

He folded the letter, and said, "I'm going to have to answer that properly," and Imaya said, "You are going to answer it properly today, and not in a month," and he said, "Yes," because she was right and because there was no version of the argument that survived contact with her in the morning.

* * *

The tea appointment was discovered by accident, in the front room, at two in the afternoon, and it was discovered by Akoja, which was worse.

"About Saturday," Akoja said, not looking up from her papers, and then looked up. "You know what, forget it. It's embarrassing for both of us."

"What's embarrassing for both of us," Zorian said.

"You agreed to it," Akoja said. "You turned up at the study group on Thursday, and you said — and I am quoting you, and I have written it down because I have learned the hard way that you do not remember agreeing to things — you said: the tea house on Vrabec Row, Saturday at three, and I'll bring the Kopriva problem with me. And then you left, and on Friday you told me I had misunderstood, and today is Saturday, and I have been thinking about this for two days, and I would like one of the two versions of you to tell me which one is lying."

The front room had gone quiet in the way that a room of students goes quiet when something genuinely interesting happens to somebody they know. Kiana had stopped mid-sentence. Kopriva had put his tea down. Naim had not moved at all, but Naim had a face for these situations and he was using it.

Zorian said, with the calm of a man who has just been handed a problem in a form he can actually work with, "Nine."

The copy came from the yard within about four seconds, which was too fast for a man crossing a room, and too slow for someone who had already been standing just inside the door with his hand on it. He looked like Zorian, in the way that a very good copy of a very good man looks like him, with small differences that accumulate honestly: Nine had been standing in the rain in the yard since dawn, and his hair fell the wrong way, and his left sleeve was wet to the elbow because he had been under a tap, and there was a chip of plaster on his cheekbone from the shed wall.

"Before you ask," Nine said: "yes, I asked her. No, I did not tell you. Yes, I knew you'd be annoyed. And I have thought about it for two days as well, and I'd still do it, because you were asleep and it was a good offer and I have nothing to do on Saturdays."

"You used my name to arrange a meeting."

"I used my face," Nine said. "It's the same face and you're the one who made it."

Akoja said, "I am going to need one of you to sit down and say the sentence properly," and so Zorian sat down, and Nine stayed standing, and the room had arranged itself into the shape of an audience, which was a disaster but not as big a disaster as the alternative.

"There are two of us in this house," Zorian said, "and one more in Koth, and this is not a trick and it is not a joke, and the one who asked you to tea is the one who is standing up. He is a copy of me. He is not a servant and he is not a disguise, and I did not know he had asked, and I am not going to punish him for it in front of you, and if I have just made this worse for you then tell me and I'll deal with that too."

Akoja looked at Nine. Nine looked at Akoja, and then, because he was a copy of a person who had once spent a month refusing to make eye contact with anyone, he looked at the ceiling, which is the sort of thing he did for the same reasons.

"Saturday," Akoja said, "at three."

"Saturday," said Nine, "at three."

"If you are going to bring the Kopriva problem," Akoja said, "I want it in writing, and I want it from whichever one of you is going to be at the table, because I have now been lied to twice by the same face and I would like to know which face owes me an apology."

"The one who lied," Zorian said. "That's me. I told you on Friday that you'd misunderstood, and I hadn't checked, and not checking was my mistake."

"That," Akoja said, "is the first useful sentence you have said about this. Nine: sit down. Both of you: I want the Kopriva problem by Monday, and if either of you tells me again that I have misunderstood something I have read twice, I will leave, and I will not come back, and you will not be able to explain me to the board."

Nine sat down. Akoja went back to her papers, and the room came back to life, and Kopriva said, into the general resumption, "I have been made into a problem, and I would like it noted that I was not consulted."

"You are a problem," Raynie said from the doorway, "and you have always been consulted."

* * *

What followed was not a fight. It was worse than a fight, which was a negotiation, conducted in the yard, in the rain, between two people with identical handwriting and identical memories up to a point, and it was conducted in front of Kirielle, who had come out to feed the chickens and had very deliberately not gone back in.

"You are not going to spend my mana on your errands," Zorian said.

"I am your mana," Nine said. "That's the whole arrangement. Every spell I cast comes out of you, and every hour I stand here costs you, and I know that because I can feel the edge of you the way you can feel the edge of me, and the reason I have not been casting anything is that I know what it costs, which is more than you know, because I'm the one standing in the rain."

That was not a fair thing to say, and it was not untrue, and Zorian spent four seconds deciding which of those two facts he was going to respond to and ended up responding to neither.

"There are three of us in the world," he said, "and there will not be four. I did the arithmetic again last week and the arithmetic is not generous. There is a number of us that we can keep standing, and it is that number, and it is not a preference and it is not stinginess, it is that above that number I cannot defend either of you and I cannot defend this house, and if I cannot do those two things then I am a danger to everybody in it. And I am not going to make another one and then spend him the way I spent people during the war."

Nine said, "I know what you spent."

"So do I."

"I'm not asking for a fifth," Nine said. "I'm asking for a Saturday, and I'm asking for you not to tell me that I asked for a thing I'm not allowed to want, because I want it, and you made me wanting it, and the least you can do is not pretend that part is an accident."

The rain was steady and patient. Somewhere on the far side of the yard wall, on Vrabec Row, a bell rang, and Zorian heard it, and stopped, because the bell was a quarter-chime and it had come from the direction of the bakery, and the bakery's bell did not do that, and the worst part was that his own internal clock had answered it a full minute early.

Kirielle said, "What's that," and Zorian said, "A bell," and Kirielle said, "That's not a quarter hour," and then, because she was eleven and because she had been paying attention all morning, "Did it ring before or after you heard it."

He looked at her, and at Nine, and at the wall, and made a decision about the size of the next step, and it was the right kind of decision, which is to say that it was too small and he knew it was too small and he took it anyway because it was honest.

"Nobody knows," Zorian said. "That's the answer to the next question you're going to ask, and the one after that. Come and put your boots on. We're walking down Vrabec Row and we're going to ask people what their bells are doing, and we are going to write the answers down, and we are not going to touch anything."

"Nine can come," Kirielle said.

"Nine can come," Zorian said, and Nine said, "I'll get the notebook," and left, and Akoja's voice came out of the open window behind him: "You have a whole street to yourselves and not one minute of your afternoon, and if you two come back with a story instead of a plan I'm going to be insufferable about it."

"Then we'll come back with a plan," Kirielle called, and the two of them went out into the rain, the original and the copy, and the copy carried the notebook, and Kirielle carried the pencil, and nobody in the household said anything about the fact that there was now a third person walking down the street with her brother's face, because Imaya had already decided that she would deal with that in the evening, and the evening was a promise on a board by the door, and this house kept its promises.

* * *

Vrabec Row was a short street of tall houses that had been a good street before the attack and was now trying to become one again, and the way you could tell which houses were succeeding was whether their doors had been replaced with the right kind of door.

The bakery's door was new. The woman who owned it was standing on her step in a apron over her dress, and she had the flat, furious, exhausted look of a person who has been told four times that she is mistaken and is about to be told a fifth.

Her name was Aneta, and she had lived in that house for nineteen years, and she did not want to talk about it, and she talked about it anyway, which was the usual shape of these things.

Forty seconds, she said, twice. She had been at her bench. She had been at her bench, and she had been at her bench, and between the two times there was nothing to say about it, and her hands knew this in a way that her head had not caught up with, because her hands had been dusted with flour to the wrist the first time and to the elbow the second, and her dough was on the wrong side of the bowl, and her kettle, which she had not boiled, was boiling, and the smell was wrong, and the smell was the part that had made her scream.

"It happened once," she said. "I want that written down. It happened once and it lasted forty seconds and I have lived in this house for nineteen years and I have never once in my life been drunk, or faint, or asleep."

"Your bell," Zorian said, "does it run true."

Aneta went inside, and came out with her husband, and her husband had a face like a shutter and a voice like a man who has been arguing about his wife's sanity in public all morning and has lost.

"It's a minute," the baker said. "In that house it's a minute and I have wound that bell for eleven years and I have never known it to be a minute, and I have taken it off the wall and cleaned it and I have put a second clock in the kitchen and the second clock says the same thing, and the man from the ward office came and told me to have her seen by a leech."

"I would like to look at the kettle," Zorian said.

"You would like to look at my kettle."

"I would like to look at your kettle, and your clock, and your bench, and the wall behind your bench, and I would like to ask if anything in this house has been bought secondhand in the last year, and if you'll allow my sister to hold the pencil, because she writes neater than either of us."

The baker looked at Kirielle, and at the copy, and at the copy again, and then he looked at Zorian with an expression that had finally, after a long and terrible morning, become interested.

"There's a third one of you," the baker said.

"Two of us at the moment," Zorian said, "and one in Koth. Your street's problem is my problem, and I don't know what it is yet, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise."

Aneta said, "Get in my house," and they got in her house.

* * *

What he did not do, standing in a baker's kitchen with a minute of wrongness sitting inside the plaster of an exterior wall, was what he had done a hundred times in the month that nobody in this city remembered: reach in with both hands of his mind, find the seam, and tear.

He could feel it. That was the horror of it, and the usefulness. The seam was small and local and it was not magic he recognized, and his own clock — that wrecked, trained, stupid clock he had built out of repetition and needed for dimensional work — had answered it a minute early, which meant that the field was running on a frame that his body had been taught to hear the way a sailor hears weather.

He also knew, with the same flat certainty, that he had no instrument, no permission, and no witness who could repeat what he found. The object that was holding the field was in the wall, and if he opened the wall the field would break, and if the field broke the forty seconds would stop, and Aneta would go back to being a woman who had been faint once, and the object would be gone, and the next house on the list would get its own minute.

"Nobody touch the plaster," Zorian said, "and nobody tell the ward office I was here. Kirielle: the wall, from the outside, all the way down, and tell me every place it's been opened. Nine: go find out who sold this house its secondhand goods this year and write down names, and if you cannot find one name, write down that you could not find one, and do not make one up."

"That's a plan," Kirielle said, with enormous satisfaction.

"That's a plan," Zorian said, and it was, and it was also too small, and he could feel the minute sitting in his family's wall, and outside, on the wet street, at a house three doors down that he had not yet reached, a kettle that nobody had put on the fire began, very quietly, to boil.
