# Chapter 1: After the Moon

The street Zorian meant to take home had acquired a hole since the afternoon.

He stopped at the rope stretched between two barrels and looked beyond it. Three work lamps lit a section of torn paving. Wooden braces leaned against the adjoining building, whose upper windows were dark. In the exposed cellar, someone was striking metal at irregular intervals.

Cyoria had become very good at putting ropes around things. There were ropes around unsafe houses, unexploded ammunition, heaps of salvaged belongings, and perfectly usable stretches of pavement that happened to be convenient places to store timber. People had begun to distinguish these categories by the enthusiasm with which they ducked under the barriers.

Zorian could go around. He could also go home without using the streets at all, but he had spent the evening walking and was reluctant to admit that the last few hundred yards required magical assistance.

Then the hammering stopped.

A woman climbed halfway out of the cellar opening and turned toward someone behind her.

"Don't touch the wheel."

She said it quietly enough that Zorian almost continued on his way.

"I haven't," a man answered from below.

The woman reached the pavement. She wore a work coat with its cuffs rolled back, a copper measuring instrument hanging from a strap, and enough pale dust to make the color of her hair uncertain. She looked at the braces, then at a narrow window near the ground.

Something cracked beneath the street.

One of the work lamps tilted. The woman caught it before it fell into the opening, set it down, and shouted a name.

This time Zorian did not consider taking the longer route.

He stepped over the rope as a young laborer scrambled from the cellar. The boy was holding a wrench in both hands, although he seemed to have forgotten it was there. Behind him came a deeper grinding sound.

Zorian extended his senses.

Two minds nearby: the woman, focused and alarmed, and the boy, whose fear was much less organized. A third lay below the pavement, frightened and in pain. Farther away, behind the wall of the adjoining house, there were sleeping people.

No swarm of hostile minds. No prepared ambush that he could detect. No sudden cascade of unfamiliar wards closing around him.

A building was trying to fall over. That was all.

The distinction was oddly reassuring.

"How many below?" he asked.

The woman glanced at him. "One. You need to stand back."

"And in the house?"

"Two on the top floor. We had the lower rooms cleared. Can you fetch the watch?"

"He can." Zorian pointed to the boy. "Can you cast?"

The woman straightened. Her first assessment of him was being revised, though not by very much.

"Enough to know this is not a place for a student to practice."

Another crack traveled through the cellar. A powdery line appeared in the mortar above the narrow window.

Zorian raised one hand, shaping a force brace beneath the nearest loaded beam. He did it slowly enough for the woman to see the spell take form. Beneath it, out of her sight, three smaller fields caught the moving stonework and distributed its weight across the surviving supports.

The grinding ceased.

"The watch," he repeated to the boy. "Tell them someone's trapped, and the neighboring house needs to be emptied."

The woman nodded once. "Go. South corner. Don't stop at the cart office; they're shut."

The boy ran.

Zorian knelt at the cellar opening. The man below was alive and conscious. A short divination gave him an incomplete view of a slab leaning across a passage, a broken wooden frame, and a leg beneath it. The surviving wardwork distorted the spell badly enough to blur distances.

He could force a clearer image through it. He could also tear the entire obstruction away and carry the man into the street. The difficulty with the latter was not the weight; it was the number of other objects resting on it.

"What happened?"

"We were unloading the temporary support," the woman said. "Nothing should have moved until we released the lower shoe. Dovin was checking the seating."

She crouched beside him and directed her lamp into the opening.

"Dovin! Can you hear me?"

"I'm not dead," a muffled voice answered. "I'd mention it if I was."

Some of the tension left her face.

"Keep talking."

"About what?"

"Anything that isn't advice."

She put the lamp down and looked at Zorian's raised hand. "How long can you hold that?"

Longer than she would believe, provided the rest of the building did not decide to join in.

"Long enough to get him out. Which part of the frame is supposed to carry the load?"

She drew a quick shape in the dust with her finger. Four upright supports, two crosspieces, a pair of circles at the base.

"These are temporary. The original cellar arch is here. The front of the house lost its footing during the attack. We transferred the weight to the frame while the masons rebuilt beneath it. Tonight we were meant to put it back."

"Meant to?"

"The new footing passed its test this afternoon. The frame passed at sunset. Then the south anchor took more than its share, the north one unloaded, and the lower beam turned under Dovin."

Zorian looked at the drawing. The design was straightforward. There were better ways of doing it, but none that would have helped if the people maintaining it could not reproduce them.

"Where's the feed?"

"Disconnected."

He glanced at her.

She reached beneath her coat and held up a thick ceramic key. One end was streaked with copper.

"Disconnected," she repeated. "Before we started."

There was still mana moving beneath the street.

Not much. It could have been the frame's reserve bleeding through a damaged regulator. Except the flow was too orderly, rising in one section, falling, then rising in another.

"Don't reconnect it," he said.

"I wasn't planning to."

He lowered himself into the cellar.

* * *

The air below was warm and tasted of wet plaster. A lamp hung from an iron hook, casting more shadow than light. Dovin lay on his side beyond a tilted slab, with one arm bent awkwardly beneath him and his left boot pinned under the fallen crosspiece.

Zorian reinforced the visible brace and let the other fields hold steady. There were places here where a little power did more than a great deal of power applied indiscriminately. Unfortunately, deciding which places those were required information.

"Can you move your toes?" he asked.

Dovin regarded him through a coating of dust.

"Are you a healer?"

"No."

"Then that's a discouraging question."

The man moved his foot slightly and hissed.

"Yes."

Good. The injury was still an injury, but at least the beam had caught the thick sole of his boot against the floor rather than crushing his entire ankle.

Zorian studied the obstruction. He would need to lift the slab, shift the beam, and keep the remaining frame from rotating. He could do all three himself. He would also have to explain why he had remained comfortably conversational while doing so.

Footsteps sounded on the cellar stairs. The woman came down carrying a short iron jack and a bundle of wedges.

"I told the people upstairs to use the back stairs. The watch is coming."

"You're going to put that under the beam?"

"Under the slab. If we lift the beam first, we pull out the last thing keeping this end of the arch from spreading."

Zorian checked. She was right. The apparent joint at the wall was not a joint at all, but an old repair hidden beneath plaster. He could have caught the result when it moved, but he preferred not to learn structural details by listening to them break.

"Fine. I'll keep it steady."

"What's your name?"

"Zorian."

"Mara Vesk. You lose your grip on that spell, Zorian, you tell me before you try something clever."

She did not wait for an answer. She knelt beside Dovin, checked his arm, and began fitting the jack beneath the slab. Her movements were quick without looking hurried. At one point she stopped to brush grit off the bearing plate before setting it down again.

Zorian used the time to examine the anchors.

The northern one was a squat block of dark stone set into the cellar floor. A new copper collar encircled it. Most of the visible formula was ordinary load-sharing work, designed to make two supports behave as a single structure. Someone had cut channels into the stone to secure the collar and filled the gaps with a conductive paste.

Below the collar, partly concealed by a crust of old mineral deposits, lay another inscription.

He could not read enough of it to determine its purpose. There were two curved channels and a connecting groove that disappeared beneath the floor. Whatever it was, the stone had not originally been made for this frame.

Reused material. Half of Cyoria was being repaired with reused material.

Mara began turning the jack.

The stone's mana flow rose.

Zorian felt it first in the supports. His hidden fields tightened as the load shifted. The north anchor went slack, not all at once but in three distinct steps. Then the southern one answered.

"Stop."

Mara's hand came off the handle.

The slab moved another fraction of an inch. Dust streamed from its upper edge. Zorian increased the force beneath it, arresting the movement before the frame could twist.

Below the copper collar, the old groove shone faintly.

"What did you do?" Mara asked.

"Nothing to the anchors."

She looked from him to the block, then took the measuring instrument from its strap. Two thin needles rested behind a scratched glass cover. She placed its probe against the copper.

One needle moved. The other stayed still.

"There's a reserve somewhere," she said.

"How large?"

"There shouldn't be one at all. Not on this side of the disconnect."

Dovin made a small, unhappy sound.

"I would like to discuss that outside."

"So would I," Mara said.

Zorian traced the newer formula with his eyes. It was trying to balance the two supports against a changing input. The dangerous part was not that it was failing to compensate. It was that, for short intervals, it succeeded. Every successful correction transferred the weight to the wrong place.

He could sever the connection. The uncertainty lay in what the older inscription would do when deprived of an outlet.

No need to find out with a man's foot underneath it.

"Can you give the jack a second bearing point? There, against that course of stone."

"That's a drain cover."

"Under it."

Mara looked, then reached past the cover and tapped the masonry with the handle of her probe. The sound changed.

"Yes. If you keep the slab still."

She shifted the jack. Zorian drew a thread of mana along the new collar, mapping the response without feeding power into it. The old inscription reacted only weakly. The connection was narrower than the collar made it appear: a pair of contacts underneath the northern edge.

He could bridge the current around the failing regulator, hold it there long enough to remove the man, then isolate the frame physically.

He disliked improvising around unidentified spellwork. He disliked it considerably less than letting someone remain beneath a slab while he arranged a proper laboratory.

"When I say, turn the jack twice. Then stop."

"It's taking weight already."

"I know."

She looked up at him. For the first time, something besides urgency entered her expression.

He formed the bypass.

The northern anchor resisted for an instant. Then its fluctuating draw settled into a steady flow. Across the cellar, the southern collar answered with a small metallic click.

"Now."

Mara turned the handle. Once. Twice.

The slab rose.

Zorian held it while she slid the wedges into place. He released the smallest of his hidden fields, confirming the load had settled where he expected. Nothing moved.

"Beam next," he said.

"Slowly."

He lifted it just far enough for Dovin to pull his boot free. The man started to scramble backward, but Mara caught his shoulder.

"Your arm. Don't put weight on it."

Between them they got him clear of the frame. Zorian lowered the beam onto an empty patch of floor, where it landed with an undignified thump.

For a few seconds nobody spoke.

Dovin looked at his foot, then at the beam.

"I want it recorded," he said, breathing hard, "that I never touched the wheel."

Mara sat back on her heels.

"It will be the first line."

* * *

By the time the watch arrived, Dovin was sitting on an overturned crate in the street and arguing with a healer about his arm. Two residents from the upper floor stood in nightclothes beside a laundry basket. Someone had brought them a blanket; someone else had begun asking whether the empty building next door was safe.

Zorian remained in the cellar until the new shoring had been secured. The watch had brought a mage who understood enough of the frame to confirm the temporary supports were sound. Under his supervision, the workers removed the copper contacts and placed dry insulating plates beneath them.

Only then did Zorian let the bypass dissolve.

The newer formula went dark.

The older stone pulsed twice.

He bent closer.

First a brief rise along one curve. Then a weaker response along the other. The connecting groove carried something away beneath the floor. The interval was short, less than a second, but unmistakable once he knew to watch for it.

"Residual charge?" the watch mage asked.

"Possibly."

"How long have you been feeding it?"

"I was feeding the bypass. Not the stone."

The man peered down without enthusiasm. He had a tired face and plaster in the folds of his robe. Zorian suspected this was not his first failed ward of the night.

"We'll have the artificers look at it. No one goes down here until then."

It was a reasonable decision. Zorian found himself irritated by it anyway.

He could spend an hour here, map the hidden inscription, and probably determine where the pulse was going. He could also spend an hour here and discover three more things that each required a day of work. If he pursued every oddity in post-invasion Cyoria, he would eventually become part of the city's permanent fixtures.

He followed the mage up the stairs.

Mara was at the barrier, writing on a board propped against a barrel. She had marked two times on her cuff in black pencil. One was the failure; the other, judging by the interval, was Dovin's extraction.

"Surname?" she asked.

"Why?"

She turned the board so he could see it.

The first line read: Dovin reports no movement of release wheel before failure.

Below it was an account of what had happened, remarkably free of speculation. The two disconnected anchors. The change in loading. The rescue. Zorian's assistance.

"Because if I write 'a passing boy held it up,' someone will decide he was a passing boy I invented."

"Kazinski."

She wrote it down. Her pencil stopped halfway through the last letters.

"The spiders?"

"Not all of them."

She gave him a flat look.

"I meant the student working with House Aope."

"Yes."

He felt her attention change. There was no sudden wall of fear, which was pleasant, but a little caution sharpened the edges of her thoughts. She looked back at the cellar opening.

"Did you find Dovin that way?"

"I could tell someone was alive below."

She wrote another line.

"You don't need to put that in."

"Would you rather I put in that I knew he was conscious because I could hear him complaining?"

"That would also be true."

For the first time she smiled.

"It would."

She crossed out the unfinished line and replaced it with something shorter. Zorian read the whole account before signing. It said he had maintained magical support during the extraction and assisted in isolating the damaged frame. It did not claim he had designed the frame, inspected it beforehand, or understood why it failed.

There were worse documents to have his name on.

"Can you copy this?" Mara asked.

He looked at her.

"The office will want my original. Dovin will want to know what he said. And I would like to retain a version that hasn't acquired three additional pages describing what I should have done."

She had spare paper in a waxed folder. He made three copies, carefully matching the ink rather than the dirt on the page.

Mara watched his hands. "That is useful."

"More useful than holding up stone?"

"It comes up more often."

She gave him one copy.

"You can keep that. If someone asks about your part in this, don't try to remember the times. Use what we wrote."

Across the street, the healer helped Dovin stand. He tested his foot with exaggerated caution, accepted a walking stick, and called that he expected the morning off.

"You may have it," Mara called back.

"Paid?"

"Don't become ambitious."

The healer said something to her, and she nodded. No broken ankle. The arm would need treatment, but Dovin would be going home rather than to a ward full of people hurt in the invasion.

Zorian was surprised by the relief he felt at that. There had been a problem, and now there was one less injured person beneath a building. No larger qualification was necessary.

He folded the report.

"The stone still has a flow through it," he said. "The older part. Have whoever inspects it measure twice. The second response is weaker."

Mara's pencil paused above her cuff.

"How much weaker?"

"I didn't measure it precisely."

"But separate?"

"Yes."

She took out a small notebook and wrote that down too.

"Which Academy instructor would you recommend for a dispute about a load ward?"

"What kind of dispute?"

"The kind in which the stone cannot possibly have done what people saw it do."

He considered it. "Ilsa Zileti could probably direct you to someone. She's been doing reconstruction work."

"And where do I find you if the office wants the witness?"

He could have named the Academy, but it was closed, and his association with Imaya's house was hardly a secret. He gave her the address.

"Not before breakfast," he added.

"What time do students eat breakfast?"

"Later than construction workers."

"That doesn't narrow it down."

He left her beside the barrier, measuring the distance between the cellar opening and a mark on the opposite wall. The crack in the pavement was no longer widening. Behind him, the watch mage told a curious passerby that there was nothing to see.

This was, at last, approximately true.

* * *

Imaya's house was not quiet when he arrived, but it was making an effort.

Someone whispered fiercely behind a closed door. A chair scraped overhead, stopped, and scraped again with the deliberate caution of a person certain that doing something slowly made it silent. Light showed beneath the kitchen door.

Zorian took off his shoes and discovered that the left one contained enough grit to establish a small quarry.

"Leave those there," Imaya said from the kitchen. "And don't use a cleaning spell in the hall. The last person who tried sent the dust under every door."

"That sounds like a poorly designed spell."

"Then they can discuss the design with the people washing their bedding."

He left the shoes.

Imaya had a ledger open on the table. Beside it sat three empty cups, a basket of mending, and a plate covered with another plate. She looked at his clothes without appearing particularly surprised.

"I saved you something. Is that blood?"

Zorian examined his sleeve. "No. Rust."

"Good. Sit down before you put it on a doorframe."

There was stew under the second plate. It was still warm.

He had not been hungry while holding up the cellar. He was now.

Imaya turned a page in her ledger. "Your sister tried to wait up. I sent her upstairs. If she tells you I promised she could stay awake until you came back, I didn't."

"What did she want?"

"To know where you were. Then she remembered she was annoyed with you about something, and wanted to know where you were so she could tell you."

"That sounds familiar."

He ate several mouthfuls. Imaya did not immediately ask about the dust, which was considerate of her. He could have said he had helped with a reconstruction problem and left it there. Instead, he found himself explaining the trapped worker, the unstable frame, and the disagreement between what a support was supposed to do and what it had actually done.

She listened while pairing stockings.

"And the man got out?"

"Yes."

"Then you can stop holding your spoon like that."

He looked down. His fingers were still in roughly the position he had used to maintain the visible force brace.

He changed his grip.

"I'm not worried about the man. There's something wrong with the anchor."

"Is anyone going to sleep under it?"

"No. The building's empty. The watch is keeping people out."

"And are you going back tonight?"

He considered the question for a little too long.

Imaya put the paired stockings in her basket.

"Your stew will be cold."

He resumed eating.

On the corner of the ledger he noticed a list of household expenses. Extra bedding. Lamp oil. Repairs to a pump. A surprisingly large amount spent on onions. Individually the figures were small. Together they amounted to a sum he would once have noticed and had lately begun noticing again.

He could have paid it without difficulty. He could have paid several such lists. That was not his problem.

His problem was that he kept measuring expenses against the money currently in his possession, rather than the money he could expect to have a month later. By that method, every sensible purchase remained sensible right up until the moment he ran out of money.

"Have we been paying enough for the extra meals?" he asked.

Imaya glanced at him.

"Yes."

"The study group uses the kitchen rather a lot."

"So I've noticed."

"I could contribute something for—"

"You could tell me how many people to expect tomorrow. That would contribute more than another apology about the chairs."

He did not actually know. Some people came every day. Others appeared whenever they heard Ilsa might be teaching. If Raynie left as planned, Kiana might find something else to occupy her, or she might arrive and complain about her absence for an hour. Neither outcome seemed safely predictable.

"I'll ask Akoja to keep a list."

"Thank you."

It was a very small request. There was no reason for it to feel like something he had neglected for weeks.

He finished the stew and took the plates to the basin.

"Zorian?"

He turned.

"If you're going to work nights, tell me. I don't mind keeping food for you. I do mind deciding whether to send someone to look."

"I wasn't working. I was walking home."

"Then try to get better at it."

Upstairs, Kirielle was asleep with one arm outside her blanket. The snow globe he had bought her stood on the nightstand, beside a folded sheet of paper and Kosjenka. The little golem doll was still.

He cleaned his clothes properly before entering, shut the door, and put the report in his desk drawer. The hinges gave a small click.

Kirielle opened her eyes.

"You were out for ages."

"You were supposed to be asleep."

"I was. Then you woke me up."

He had no good answer to that.

She pushed herself upright, rubbing one eye. "Did you bring me anything?"

"Some very interesting dust."

"That's not funny."

"It's the only thing I found in abundance."

She considered whether this improved his answer, then reached for the folded paper. It contained a drawing of her snow globe. Beside it, she had drawn three smaller circles and a tangle of arrows.

"I did the lines."

He took the sheet. Earlier, he had asked her to draw what she thought she was doing while trying to guide a tiny amount of mana through a simple exercise. It was an attempt to discover whether they meant the same thing by words such as thin and steady. Kirielle was quite capable of telling him she understood an instruction and then doing something unrelated with considerable confidence.

The arrows were not what he had expected.

"Why does it go around here?"

"Because if I push straight, it wobbles."

"And this?"

"That's where I stopped. You said I should stop if it got difficult."

He had. She had apparently interpreted this as permission to stop almost immediately.

Still, the drawing told him more than another ten minutes of asking whether she could feel the difference.

"We can try it that way tomorrow. No practice now."

She gave him an offended look. "I wasn't going to."

"Good."

"When tomorrow?"

"After breakfast."

"You said that yesterday. Then the people came."

He remembered promising to look over her exercise and then being intercepted by a question about someone else's shaping. That had become several questions, and then a demonstration, and then it had somehow been time to leave.

"Before the group starts," he said. "Half an hour. You get the chair by the window."

"And nobody can take it?"

"Within reason."

"What reason?"

"The house catching fire."

Kirielle thought about that.

"Fine. But if it's only a little fire, I still get the chair afterward."

He set the drawing beside his papers. "Go back to sleep."

She lay down, then immediately lifted her head again.

"Zach said I should ask whether you've checked under your bed."

Zorian looked at the bed.

There was nothing beneath it except a storage box, a missing slipper, and the normal accumulation of dust. No enchantment. No altered object. No living creature larger than an ordinary spider, which was making commendably little effort to involve itself in his life.

Kirielle began to giggle.

"He said you'd look."

"How much has he been paying you?"

"He hasn't."

"Then you should renegotiate."

That only made her laugh harder. He waited until she settled, extinguished the brighter lamp, and sat at his desk with a small light shielded beneath his hand.

Zach had not stopped being his friend. Sometimes that made the anger easier to bear. Sometimes it meant Zorian could tell himself that the pranks were all that remained of it, which was convenient and almost certainly untrue.

He would talk to him. Preferably at a time when neither of them had a public audience or a useful distraction.

Tomorrow was beginning to look crowded.

He took out a blank sheet. At the top he wrote the things that needed doing in the morning. Kirielle. The study group. A proper account of his expenses. Under that he placed the more difficult things: inspect Mrva's storage, review the next batch of research gifts, find a way of making money that did not advertise his ability to people with enough spare time to investigate it.

He paused over Princess, then added her too.

The list looked less like a plan than an accusation.

He turned the sheet over and drew the cellar anchor.

That was much more relaxing.

The collar was easy. The two lower curves were less so. He had seen only parts of them, through dirt and poor light, and he refused to fill in the missing sections merely because one arrangement seemed aesthetically pleasing. He marked the visible lines firmly and left gaps where his observations ended.

There was a temptation to call this old magic and become excited about it. There was an equally strong temptation to call it incompetent salvage work and forget it. Neither was evidence.

He recorded the two pulses. The second had been weaker, but he had not timed them accurately, and his own spellwork had been active nearby. Any serious examination would have to begin without his interference.

That was a useful stopping point.

He put the pencil down.

For several seconds he continued looking at the drawing. Then he covered it with Kirielle's sheet, where the arrows ran crookedly around a snow globe, and went to bed.

* * *

In the morning, the chair by the window was occupied by a pile of textbooks.

Kirielle arrived carrying two pieces of paper, saw the obstruction, and drew a breath.

"They're mine," Zorian said. "They're moving."

She watched until he had relocated the pile, then climbed into the chair with the expression of someone taking possession of contested territory.

The kitchen smelled of bread and hot onions. Kael sat at the far end of the table, reading a list of fittings for his new laboratory. He looked up when Zorian shifted a bowl to make room for Kirielle's drawings.

"You're up early."

"I was threatened."

"I asked," Kirielle corrected.

"Repeatedly."

Kael glanced at the drawings. "That seems to work better than my method."

"Your method is asking me about spells while I'm carrying something."

"You can't walk away as quickly."

Zorian pushed a cup away from Kirielle's elbow. "Nobody is casting anything at the table. We're comparing drawings."

"I know," she said. "You already said."

It turned out that the largest circle on her first sheet did not represent the mana she was guiding. It represented her hand. This explained a great deal about the arrows and made two of his intended corrections unnecessary.

He asked her to draw the exercise again, this time with the hand separate. She bent over the page, serious enough to forget she had won an argument about the chair.

Kael returned to his list. Outside the window, a cart creaked past. For a few minutes nothing required rescuing.

The knock came before Kirielle had finished.

She looked up at once.

"Keep going," Zorian said. "I promised."

Imaya went to the door. He heard a woman's voice in the hallway and recognized Mara before she appeared.

She had washed the dust from her face and hair, which was brown. The cuffs of her work coat still carried the previous night's times. She held a folded drawing under one arm and a paper packet in her hand.

"You said after breakfast," she said when she saw him.

Zorian looked at the plates on the table. It was difficult to argue that they had not eaten.

"And I've promised my sister the next few minutes."

Mara looked at Kirielle, who had planted her elbows over both drawings.

"Then I'll wait."

Kirielle considered this unexpected answer, nodded, and returned to work.

Imaya found another cup. Mara sat near the door, unrolled her drawing without opening it fully, and occupied herself with checking a column of figures. She did not interrupt. Zorian found this more persuasive than if she had explained at length how little time she needed.

They finished the comparison. Kirielle had been attempting to control the entire exercise at once, rather than guiding a small portion and letting the rest follow. He explained the distinction with a strand of thread, which she understood immediately and then accused him of not explaining properly before.

She was not entirely wrong.

He assigned her a short supervised practice for later, returned the drawings, and made sure she knew the lesson had ended because they had finished rather than because somebody more important had arrived.

"Can I keep the thread?"

"Yes."

"Good."

She left with the papers tucked under one arm and the thread wound around a finger. In the hallway, she began telling someone that Zorian had finally made the exercise sensible.

Mara waited until the footsteps had moved away.

"Dovin's home. His arm will mend. He wants me to thank you, but not in a way that encourages you to charge him."

"I'm glad we agree on the appropriate scale of gratitude."

She pushed the packet across the table.

Inside was a thin flake of copper stuck to a dark crust. Zorian did not touch it.

"From the collar?"

"The piece that came off when the watch removed the contacts. Their mage let me keep it. I've written down which face it came from."

"Why bring it to me?"

"Because the works office says the anchor was idle when we began. They have a certified reading. They would like to know what my crew did to change that."

"They can inspect the stone."

"They can. They've locked the site and scheduled an inspection. Meanwhile, they've suspended my authority to unload the remaining frames."

"That sounds sensible if this one failed."

"It is sensible. What isn't sensible is leaving the other crews with readings taken by the same method and telling them those readings are beyond question."

She opened the drawing.

It showed a line of streets, six marked cellars, and the position of last night's accident. Beside one of the other sites she had drawn a small pair of peaks.

Zorian leaned forward.

"You checked another anchor."

"From the street access. I didn't alter anything."

"Did it respond twice?"

"Yes."

She laid out a second sheet: a rough trace from an instrument, accompanied by written times and settings. The peaks were too regular to be random interference. Her instrument did not resolve them very well, but they were there.

"This one has the feed disconnected too?"

"Since yesterday afternoon. No workers below it. I've notified the watch and told the morning crew to stay out."

Good. She had not left someone beneath a suspect structure while coming to find a more interesting opinion.

He studied the trace. At the first cellar, the weaker peak had followed the stronger one.

Here it came first.

"Could your instrument have reversed the order?"

"That was my first question. I checked against a known discharge. Twice."

Kael set his list aside.

Zorian was already reaching for a pencil.

Mara put a hand on the edge of the drawing.

"Before you do anything: I'm not asking you to certify my work. I don't need someone who will tell the office I was right because I was kind to his sister. I need someone to explain this well enough that another mage can test it. You said Miss Zileti could help. Will you introduce me?"

He looked at the column of instrument settings. Mara had recorded the things she did not know as carefully as the ones she did. That was rarer than it ought to have been.

"Yes," he said. "And I may be able to help with the measurement."

"Under whoever she recommends. Properly."

"I understood."

"There's a diagnostic allowance. Not a large one. I can have it assigned to whoever does the work, if the office accepts the scope."

He had been about to say that the money was unnecessary.

The list on the other side of his anchor drawing came back to him: storage, repairs, paper, reagents, food. A whole collection of unremarkable costs waiting behind the remarkable ones.

"Then we should agree on the scope," he said.

Mara lifted her hand from the drawing.

Zorian placed his account of the cellar beside her measurements. He had wanted a clean reading without his own interference. Here was one, from a stone he had never touched.

The reversed order did not prove that the two anchors were communicating. It did not prove that either pulse had caused the failure. There were several ordinary explanations, and he would have to eliminate them before indulging in interesting ones.

But he no longer thought the second pulse was merely something left over.

He drew a question mark between the two sites.

"Let's find out what they're connected to."
