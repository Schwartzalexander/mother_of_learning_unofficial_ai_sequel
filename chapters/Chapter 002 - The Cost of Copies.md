# Chapter 2: The Cost of Copies

Xvim got to the arithmetic before Zorian did, which was usual, and he did it at the kitchen table at seven in the morning with a pencil and no paper, which was not.

"You have made a mistake," Xvim said, "and it is a small one, and I want you to correct it before it becomes a habit."

"It's too early for a mistake," Zorian said. "Give me a bowl of something and tell me later."

"The number of you," Xvim said. "You keep three. You speak about three as though it is a natural fact, like weather, and it is not a fact, it is a bill, and you have not added it up in front of anybody since the war, and the reason you have not added it up is that you are afraid of the answer."

Imaya, at the stove, said, "Say the number in my kitchen or don't say it at all," and Xvim said, "The number is what he already knows," and Imaya said, "Then say it quietly and he'll hear it like everybody else."

Xvim had the courtesy to look slightly rebuked, and the sense to keep the pencil.

Nine was at the far end of the table with the notebook, doing Aneta's street on it in his own handwriting, and he had been up since before dawn, and there was a smudge of plaster on the same cheekbone as yesterday, which meant he had slept in his clothes again, because he could not be made to feel tired in the order the household expected.

Here is what the bill looked like, and Zorian said it out loud in his own kitchen because the alternative was having it said to him.

A copy cost mana to make, and it cost mana to keep, and the keeping was not a spell you cast once and admired. It was a draught on him that ran all day and did not stop when he slept, and it took the shape of a thinness behind the eyes and a slower recovery, and it was worse the further the copy stood from him, which was why the one in Koth was the cheapest of the three and also the hardest to talk to. Every spell a copy cast came out of the same pool, at the same price he would pay himself, and it came out of him faster, because a copy's shaping was not as clean as an original's and a good copy knows this and hates it. And the number of copies a man could hold was not a matter of willpower: it showed up in his reserves, measurably, and if he lied about it, the lie would be paid for by whoever was standing next to him when the pool ran out.

Three was not generous. Three was the number at which he could still hold a shield over a room of children, and still open a gate, and still think straight for six hours.

"There is a fourth," Zorian said, "and I will make one, and I already made one last week, and I made it for two hours and I made it in the yard and I made it do nothing but carry a crate of Mrva's brackets across the yard and put it down again, and when it went away I sat on the coal for twenty minutes and did not know why my hands were shaking, and that was two hours of one copy, in addition to the three I keep."

Kirielle said, "You didn't tell me about the coal."

"You were not supposed to see it," Zorian said.

"You don't get to decide that," Kirielle said, "because I live here."

That was a fair thing to say, and the household let it stand, which is how you can tell a household is functioning.

Xvim said, "Then stop at three."

"I'm stopping at three."

"You said that in the war," Xvim said, "and then you made four, and then six, and then I stopped counting because I did not want to be the one to say it, and each time you made one you told yourself it was the last one and each time you spent one you did not come back for it, and I am not raising this to be difficult. I am raising it because there is a thing happening on Vrabec Row that eats time in a house, and the only man in this city who can hear it is running on a thin pool with three of himself standing around him, and somebody is going to have to say that out loud before the week is done."

The kitchen had gone quiet in the specific way kitchens go quiet when a person who is loved says something unkind and correct.

"Say the other thing too," Imaya said, without turning around. "Since you've started."

Xvim said, "The other thing is that I have listened to the way you speak about them, and I have heard you say it two ways. You say he, and then you say it, in the same breath, in front of people who are watching your face to find out what to think. And the copy at the end of my table has heard it as clearly as I have, and he is better at not showing it than you are."

Nine said, "I'm not showing it," and Xvim said, "You are holding your pencil the way he holds his when he is trying not to leave a room," and Nine put the pencil down, and then picked it up again, because putting it down was a concession and he was not going to make one before he had decided what he was conceding.

Zorian said, "Both ways are wrong, and I'll fix it, and I'm not going to fix it in a speech," and Imaya said, "Good," and put a bowl in front of him, and the table went back to work.

He wrote the letter to Four before anything else, standing at the window with the board propped on the sill, and he wrote it in his own hand and did not dictate it to anyone, which is the only reason it was any good.

Four would keep the room near the Silent Doorway's house, and would tell the family what he was, and if they wanted him out he was to come to the gate and not sleep in a doorway, and that was not a request. Four would stop inventing work and would do the one real thing instead: he was to sit down with the Koth end's gate clerks and start collecting, in writing, every price and every restriction that had been put on the gate in the last three months, because prices and restrictions were the way you could see what other people were afraid of before they said so. Four would take the wedding preparations seriously; nobody was going to treat his place in that family as a detail, and Zorian was going to have to ask him, properly, whether he wanted to walk through the gate to the ceremony or stand at the Koth end and watch it from there, and he would not be asked that question by a letter, and an answer was not required yet.

And then, because it was true: Four had asked, in effect, whether a person who is a part of somebody else's family is a member of it. Zorian had no answer for that yet, and he wrote that down as the honest thing, and then under it he wrote that he was going to try to be more careful about which of them was being spoken about, and that he had been told this that morning by someone with better manners than he deserved, and that Four should expect to be addressed as himself from now on, in writing, and so should Nine.

He sealed it, and paid the postage, and the postage had indeed gone up, and Nine had been right about that and about the insult in it.

* * *

The second thing Kirielle asked for that morning was to be shown the cost, and this time he said yes, because she had asked in the right way, which was after she had heard the number.

"Stand where you can see my hands and not my face," Zorian said, in the yard, "and count out loud, and stop me if I lose count, because if I lose count then I've lost the shape and the shape is what you're here to see."

He made a copy in the yard, in the wet, in front of his sister, for the specific purpose of having it do something boring, and he made it slowly, which is the only way to make one cheaply. The mana came out of him in a long pull rather than a slam, and he felt it leave, and the copy assembled itself out of nothing much at all: light, and a suggestion of weight, and then weight, and then the clothes, and then the face, and then the thing that makes a face wrong by a quarter of a degree, which he had spent years learning to fix and which he did not fix now, because fixing that part costs more than it is worth.

The copy stood in the wet grass and looked at him with his own face at a quarter of a degree, and Kirielle counted out loud in a small voice, and got to eleven, and said, "You went red."

"I went red for a reason," Zorian said, "and the reason is not that it's hard. It's that it's expensive. Go and pick up the hammer and put it down again."

The copy did that, and it was nothing at all, and it cost him something he could feel, and he let Kirielle watch him pay for nothing at all.

"Now do you understand why I don't have six of me," he said, "and why I don't use them for your lessons, and why I don't send one of me to the Academy to sit lectures while I sleep."

"But you could," Kirielle said.

"I could put one of me in a lecture theatre, and it would learn what the instructor says, and it would come back to me, and I would know none of it," Zorian said. "That's the part that breaks people's hearts when they hear it. Whatever one of me learns, I do not get. It is not folded back into me. It has to tell me, or write it down, or teach it to me, and if it teaches it to me then I have to sit and learn it like anybody, and then what was the point, except that it will do the boring part twice and I will do it once, which is worth something and is not magic."

Kirielle considered that with the seriousness she usually saved for arguments about her own allowance.

"So the copy has to be good at teaching," she said, "or you learn badly."

"That's exactly it, and if the copy doesn't want to teach me then we have an argument, and the argument is one of the reasons I don't have six."

Kirielle looked at the new copy, which was standing in the wet with the hammer in its hand, and then she said, "What does this one want?"

It was the right question, and it was not the question he had prepared for, and he answered it honestly in front of the copy, which is the only place an answer like that counts.

"He wants to not be standing in the rain," Zorian said. "He's going to put the hammer down and carry the brackets over with Nine, and then I'm going to take him back, and taking him back is not a punishment and it isn't a dismissal, it's the end of somebody who existed for about an hour, and I am going to do it in private and I am going to remember that it happened, and you are going to see me come back out of doing it, and that is the whole lesson, and if you tell anybody at the Academy this is how it's done, they will call you a monster and they will be wrong and you should let them."

"Are you a monster," Kirielle said.

"I'm a man who does a monstrous thing carefully and gets less careful every year, which is the thing to watch," Zorian said. "Hold your bend. Both hands. Count to twenty out loud."

She did it, and he watched her wrist instead of her face, and at sixteen she lost the shape, and at twenty-one she had it, and the light came out of the yard in a small white column and held there while the new copy carried a bracket-frame across the wet grass behind it, and for the length of that light the whole yard was on the side of the useful.

* * *

The salvage sale was on a Tuesday, at a yard by the canal where three houses' worth of what had not burned was being sold by a firm with good lawyers and no taste, and Raynie had said she would shout at people, and she did, and she was good at it.

Zorian went with Nine, and Kirielle, and an inventory of things he had meant to sell and did not want to, laid out on a wet blanket: metal he had taken off his own constructs, two of the cages he had made during the month that he had kept out of sentimentality and would not name in public, a set of gate clamps that would be worth a great deal to the wrong man, and a box of clockwork from his own workshop that he had bought for repairs and would now have to buy back.

The reason he had come was the reason he had not told anybody, which was that he was hoping someone else would say the thing he had seen in Aneta's wall.

The brass regulator came up at half past ten, in a lot of junk, and it was the wrong size for anything in that lot, and it was a plate of interlocking teeth inside a ring, well made, better than Cyorian work, and it was not old: the pivots had been cut by a tool that left a mark he had never seen on a salvaged object, and it was running, and it was running about half a second slow, and it should not have been running at all, because there was no visible way to wind it.

"Lot forty-one," said the man with the catalogue, in the voice of a man reading a list of things he had already decided were worthless. "Assorted mechanism, brass, sold as seen, untested, no warranty, no explanation, and before anybody asks, no, we do not know what it is."

Nine said, quietly, "Ask him."

So Zorian asked him, out loud, in front of the yard, and the man with the catalogue said, "We do not know what it is," in a different voice from the first one, and a woman near the front of the crowd said, "They never know what it is," and laughed, and half the yard laughed with her, and the other half did not, and the half that did not was the half that had been buying brass clocks all month.

Because that was the thing he had come to see. There were three bidders in that yard who did not care what they were buying, who bid on mechanism, and who bid in the same flat way, at the same pause, with the same man in the grey coat raising his hand slightly late, as though somebody had told him the number and he was confirming it.

The regulator went for forty gold, which is a ridiculous price for a piece of brass and not nearly enough for what it was. The man in the grey coat raised his hand for it, and then, when Zorian raised his, did not go higher, which was worse than if he had.

"You've made an enemy for forty gold," Raynie said, with satisfaction, over the noise of the next lot.

"I've made an enemy for forty gold and a question," Zorian said. "The question is free."

He bought the rest of his own junk back, which cost more than the regulator, and sold the gate clamps, because the clamps were worth a great deal and he was not going to keep a thing that could open a gate lying around in a house with children in it, and then he stood in the rain with the money in his pocket and did the arithmetic of what the frame for Mrva would cost against what he had, and the gap between them was a number he could look at now, which was the only respect he could pay it.

"Write it down," he said to Nine, "and write down the grey coat's face, and if you can't get the face write down that you couldn't."

"I can get the face," Nine said. "I was in the crowd. I was behind him both times."

"Then write down where he stood, and who he looked at, and don't write down what you think he was doing."

"I'm his copy," Nine said, with a patience that Zorian recognized as his own and disliked in another mouth. "I know how he moved. He moved like a man with a list and a schedule, and men with lists and schedules don't come to a yard like this unless the yard is on a list."

Kirielle had her own contribution, which she produced at home, after supper, when she unrolled a sheet of paper on the table with the deliberate gravity of a person presenting a case to a court.

It was a drawing of the wall of Aneta's house, from the street, to scale, with a grid she had drawn herself, and she had marked every place the plaster had been opened, and she had counted bricks, and the point was not that she had done it. The point was that there were four openings, in four different years, at the same height above the ground, in four different houses on the same street, and that they were all at a height that meant the man doing the work had been standing on a ladder, and that the one nearest the corner had a new brick in it that was a quarter of an inch too long and had been ground down on one side, which is the sort of fact that only matters to somebody who has decided to look at bricks.

"You missed one," Zorian said, and she flushed, and then went and looked at the drawing again, and said, "No I didn't, that one's not opened, that one's painted over, and if it's painted over somebody painted it over on purpose, and I wrote that, look, I wrote painted."

She had written painted.

"Then it's five," Zorian said, "and five is not weather, and Nine will find out who owned the ladder, and I will find out what a thing like that needs in order to run for a year without being wound, and neither of us is going to say anything to Aneta until we know, and if the ward office comes back, Kirielle is going to answer the door, because she is better at answering doors than any of us."

Imaya said, "She is not answering the door to officials," and Kirielle said, "I answered the door to the soft man," and Imaya said, "That is exactly why I am saying it now," and the two of them settled it in four sentences, which was a speed that not long ago would have seemed impossible in that house.

* * *

The licensing desk at the ward office was in a annex of a annex, behind a corridor where a man with a stamp had been waiting cheerfully for forty years to tell people that they were in the wrong queue, and Zorian went there on Wednesday with Nine, and with a list of what he needed written by Imaya, who had decided that if he was going to spend the war's money he was going to spend it in the correct order.

He needed a shed. Not the shed behind Imaya's house, which was rented to the plumbing apprentice's ambitions and which had, in any case, no lock worth the name. He needed a licensed workshop yard with a wall, because he had three hundred gold worth of articulated metal in it and a girl of eleven who came in and out of the yard with a spoon.

The clerk who took his business was a narrow man with excellent handwriting and no interest in him at all, which made him the most useful official Zorian had met since the attack.

"Artificery outside a charter is not forbidden," the clerk said, "it is untaxed, and untaxed work in a dwelling where minors reside is a matter for a different office, so you are either going to pay me for a licence and a yard inspection or you are going to be visited by a man who will not offer you a form. The licence is nine. The inspection is four, and four more because your ward has been rebuilt since the attack. The deposit against complaints is fifteen, and it is returnable, and I want you to understand that the word returnable is doing a great deal of work in that sentence."

"Thirty-two," Zorian said.

"Thirty-two," the clerk said, "and it is not the thirty-two that will stop you. What will stop you is the schedule. If you keep a war construct in pieces in a yard, it goes on the schedule of military salvage held for repair, and that entry needs the name the body was registered under in this city, because registered bodies do not stop being registered just because the city stopped working for a month. Whose name is it."

"It was bought here, from an armourer in the sixth ward, in the spring," Zorian said. "He died in the first hour of the attack, and he never signed the transfer over, because he was dead, and there was an army of things in front of me and I did not think registration was the urgent thing."

"Then the body is registered to a dead man, and you are holding it, and the city has a file on it, and the army has a file on it, and the army's file says that anything of that size found in the districts is munitions," the clerk said, with the flat patience of a man who had said worse things to better men than Zorian that week. "For the entry I want the dead man's bill of sale and a witness who will swear the metal left his yard under his hand, and then I can enter it as civilian property held for repair by a claimant. That is a real thing you can be, and it means that if a piece goes missing you answer for it, and if a piece kills somebody you answer for it, and the schedule will say so in a font that is designed to be readable."

"Then I'll bring the bill of sale," Zorian said, and the clerk wrote the words in his own column, and the queue behind Zorian shifted, because there was a man with a metal colossus in his back yard and a form to prove it.

The clerk wrote it down, and then he looked up, because something in the room had changed and he had noticed it the way clerks notice things, which is late and exactly.

"You've brought two of yourself," the clerk said, "and I have seen the other one, so let me be plain, since I am being plain about your money. I have a column for the applicant and a column for a proxy, and I have a column for apprentices, and there is no column that I am permitted to put him in."

Nine said, "Put me in the proxy column," and the clerk said, "A proxy signs for you, which means he would be signing for you, and I would then be taking his word for the contents of your purse, and I have taken enough of those kinds of words to know what they cost," and he tapped the schedule with his pencil, and added, "You're going to have to decide what he is, in writing, and bring it to me, and I will file it, and until you do, he is a person standing in my office and I am going to address him as sir, and you are going to watch me do it and it is going to be uncomfortable, and that is the correct price for paperwork."

So Zorian sat down at the counter in the annex of a annex, in front of a queue that had grown behind him, and wrote, on the back of a licence form, in his own hand, the sentence that said what a copy of him was permitted to be when he was not present: that he was not property, that he was not a construct, that he could refuse an instruction, that he could leave, and that if he acted for Zorian he did so as himself and Zorian would answer for the results and expect to argue about them afterwards.

It was a bad document. It was the first one, and it said the true things in the wrong order, and Nine read it over his shoulder and said, in a voice low enough that the queue could not hear, "You've given me the right to leave and not the right to be paid," and Zorian said, "You're right," and wrote a second version, and signed the second version because the queue had grown and the clerk had stopped being interested and started being bored, which is a worse combination in an official.

"Thirty-two," Zorian said, and paid it, and took the receipt, and outside, in the street, Nine said: "You wrote that in front of people."

"I wrote it in front of people because it's true in front of people."

"You wrote that I could leave," Nine said. "You wrote it on a form, and the form is in a file, and the file is in a ward office, and now a man with a stamp can find out what I am by asking for a number, and there are men who will ask for numbers."

"Yes," Zorian said, "and if a man with a form comes to this city and starts asking what people are, I would rather he found a file that says the truth and a signature that says I said it out loud."

Nine was quiet for the length of the walk home, and then he said, "I'm not going to use the leaving part."

"I know," Zorian said, "and I'm not going to make you prove it either, because if I did that it would be the same as taking it away, and then the sentence on the form would be a lie, and I've written enough lies on forms this year to know how they behave afterwards."

* * *

The study group met in the front room on Wednesday evening because the front room had the best light and the least smell of plumbing, and because Ilsa had decided, with the quiet authority of a person who has read the rules, that the group would be run like a class whether the Academy was open or not.

Ilsa had forms. Ilsa always had forms, and this week she had the new ones, and she handed them out with the flat pleasure of a woman distributing ammunition.

"Third-year placement review," Ilsa said. "A board of four: two instructors, the registrar, and a man from the city who will agree with whichever instructor speaks loudest. If you fail the review you are held back, and if you are held back you pay another year, and if you cannot pay another year you are discharged with a certificate saying you completed three years of a five-year program, which is a document I have mostly seen used to prop doors."

"That's cheerful," Kiana said.

"It's accurate, and I didn't write it," Ilsa said. "Look at paragraph six."

Paragraph six said that a student could be advanced into the fourth-year courses of a discipline by demonstrating competence in that discipline before the board, instead of sitting the third year of it again, and that the list of disciplines on which demonstration was permitted was longer than the list of disciplines the Academy actually taught.

"It says demonstrate," Naim said, reading, "not examine. And the list is longer than the curriculum."

"Because the Academy knows what people learn outside its walls," Akoja said, "and pretends to be astonished when they learn it. Paragraph six was written by somebody who wants to let a few of us through and needs a rule to point at when the instructors object."

"And if you demonstrate one thing," Edwin said, "you're still a third-year in everything else, with fourth-year classes on your timetable, and the timetable will be built by a man who has never met you."

"That is the whole trap," Ilsa said, "and it is a trap you can choose to fall into, which is more than most of you get in this institution."

Zorian read paragraph six twice, because he had spent a month in a city where he had learned three disciplines from nobody and had then been obliged to pretend he had learned them from the Academy, and he knew exactly what a demonstration before a board of four was worth and exactly what it cost. He wrote the paragraph number on the corner of his copy of the form, and said nothing, and Akoja, who was watching him do it, wrote something of her own on the back of hers.

The room went through the rest of the forms for an hour, and the household listened from the stairs, because the household always listened from the stairs, and at the end Kopriva said, with the air of a man making conversation, "There is a fourth way, which is that a person who can demonstrate two disciplines may be taken on as a paid assistant in the third-year practicals at a rate that is insulting but real," and nobody asked him how he knew that, and nobody said it out loud either, and the lamp in the front room burned down while the study group did arithmetic that had nothing to do with magic.

* * *

Three things arrived on Thursday, and none of them was the one he had been waiting for, which is how the week actually turned.

The first was a notice, posted by the ward office and then by the city, and then copied onto Imaya's board by Kopriva, who thought everything should be on the board. It said that all war salvage of a mechanical, horological, or measuring character was now subject to review by a provisional office of the crown, that owners of such items were to declare them within a month, and that the office would provide, free of charge, an assessment of anything its owner believed had been damaged in the attack. It was signed with a title rather than a name, and the title was long, and the signature under it was small, neat, and unreadable, and the letterhead had the sort of plainness that costs more than decoration.

"They're going to take the regulator out of Aneta's wall," Zorian said, reading it twice, "and they're going to do it lawfully, and they're going to do it for free, and they're going to do it before I've finished asking who owns the ladder."

"The notice also says they'll assess anything you declare," Kopriva said, "which means that if you declare a thing you've handed it over, and if you don't, you've hidden it, and if you've hidden it, then when it turns up you're the man who hid it. Whoever wrote this has been thinking about people like you for a while."

"Have you been thinking about people like me," Zorian said, "or thinking about how to phrase a letter to a man like me."

"Both, all morning," Kopriva said, "which is why the phrasing is what it is. I would have signed it with a name, though. A name makes people nervous. A title makes them confident, and confidence is what you want out of a man who is about to hand over his own evidence."

The second thing was Akoja, who came to the house at four with the Kopriva problem in writing, read the notice, sat down at the table without being asked, and asked the question that had occurred to everybody in the room and been postponed by all of them.

"Who is this office," Akoja said, "and who is feeding it."

"It's a compensation office," Kopriva said. "There are six of them. This one's the one that pays out for lost property in the disaster districts, which means it has lists, and assessors, and money, and the least scrutiny of any of them, because nobody wants to argue with a man who is giving money back to a widow."

"You've read the six."

"I have read all of them. I have been paid by two of them," Kopriva said, "and I would like it on the record that I refused the second one, which I did not refuse because I am honest but because the offer was insulting, and I want you to notice that there is a difference."

The third thing was the kettle, and that came from Aneta, who came to the house herself in the evening with her husband and a basket she clearly intended as a payment and had decided at the door was also an apology.

Three doors down from her, a woman who did not have a bell had a kettle that boiled when it was not on, and had had it twice that week, and had moved the kettle to a different room, and had found it boiling in the different room, and had not told anybody because she was forty-one and had never in her life been the sort of woman who says a thing like that out loud.

And Aneta had news of a different kind, and she told it to Imaya in the kitchen, in a low voice, with her hands around a cup she had not drunk from, and the household heard about it later, and it was this: a man had come to her house the previous day, politely, with a form, and had asked her to describe the smell she had screamed about, and had written her description down word for word, and had then asked her, still politely, whether there was anything in her house that ran slow, and had looked at the clock in her hall while he asked it, and had then, when she said no, said: you are certain, because we have found two in this street already, and paid out for both.

"She said no," Imaya said, very quietly, and Aneta said, "I said no," and Imaya said, "Why," and Aneta said, "Because it was in my wall and I have lived here nineteen years and something in that wall has made me scream in my own kitchen, and I didn't know whose wall it was going to be next and I didn't know what they were going to do to my house to get it out."

"What did he say he was," Zorian said, from the doorway, and Aneta said, "He said he was an assessor, and he said the name Riedl, and he had a form for it and a receipt book and a way of saying madam that made me feel like I was the one who was sorry."

Nine, who had the notebook, wrote down the name before anybody had to ask him to, and then wrote down what Aneta said about the receipt book, and then, because he had decided something during the writing and it showed on his face, turned the page and began a new column.

"That's the ladder," Nine said. "Not a man with a ladder. A man with a form and a receipt book and a schedule."

And in the room beyond, with the board by the door and the bell going in the rain and the kettle in the kitchen doing nothing at all, nobody said anything about that for a while, because it was the sort of thing that changes the shape of a week, and there was no version of it that was reassuring, and the worst of it was the part that nobody said out loud either, which was that an assessor with a receipt book had been in that street before Zorian had, and had paid for what he found, and had written the whole thing down in a way that would still be readable in ten years.

Kirielle, who had been listening from the stairs with the air of a person trying very hard to be invisible, said, "He'll have the brick," and everyone looked at her, and she said, "The one that's too long. If he's been in the wall he'll have the brick, and if he has the brick then he'll know somebody measured it, because that's how you know somebody measured it."

That was, in fact, exactly right, and it was the first time in that house that a child had said the useful thing first, and Zorian wrote it down himself, in Nine's notebook, in his own leaning hand, at the top of a fresh page, and dated it, and under it he wrote the one line that the whole week had been walking toward, and he did not say it aloud, because saying it aloud would have made it a promise, and he was not ready to make one yet.

Outside, three doors down from Aneta's, a kettle lifted its lid on a cold stove, and the woman who heard it did not tell anybody, and the next morning she told the ward office, and by the end of the week the whole of Vrabec Row had been put onto a list, and the list had a heading on it, and the heading was a title rather than a name, and underneath the title, in a small neat hand, somebody had written a case number, and the case number was the fifth opened in that street.
